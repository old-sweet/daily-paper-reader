# {H, X, C-NOT }: CSS Constructions, Soundness, and Mutation-Based Validation

Abstract 

Formal verification of quantum circuits becomes increasingly difficult as circuit size increases, since exhaustive reasoning over the underlying Hilbert space is computationally expensive. In previous work [1], we introduced a bit-vector abstraction that separates the tracking of superpo-sition from basis-state behaviour, enabling SMT-based verification of fixed-size error-detection and entanglement circuits constructed from H, X, and C-NOT gates. This paper extends that framework in five directions. First, we generalise the correctness properties to parametrised repetition and concatenated phase-flip codes with up to 101 logical elements. Second, we ver-ify syndrome extraction for CSS codes specified by arbitrary parity-check matrices, including the Steane code, the [[15 , 7, 3]] quantum Hamming code, the [[15 , 1, 3]] quantum Reed-Muller code, and rotated surface codes with distances up to 101. The verification considers arbitrary error patterns, error detection up to a specified weight, and recovery using a classical decoder. Counterexamples are obtained for errors beyond the correction or detection capability of the respective codes. Third, we characterise the fragment of {H, X, C-NOT } circuits for which the abstraction faithfully represents the corresponding Hilbert-space semantics. We prove soundness for this fragment, demonstrate the necessity of the stated conditions through counterexamples, validate the characterisation by differential testing against state-vector simulation, and prove the six lemmas established in the original work. Fourth, we conduct a mutation study using 13 mutation operators and automatically identify equivalent mutants. Within the evaluated fault model, the approach detects all 622 non-equivalent mutants. Fifth, we implement the ap-proach directly using the Z3 API and verify GHZ circuits with up to 262,144 qubits across three topologies on a 16 GB laptop. The evaluation includes repeated measurements and a controlled ablation of the encoding pipeline and solver version. Experiments with random H+C-NOT networks show that verification cost increases steeply with circuit depth when outputs depend on a large number of inputs. The approach is not intended to cover general stabiliser circuits, Y -type stabilisers, fault-tolerant protocols, or physical noise. We identify the extensions required to address these cases. 

Keywords: Bell state, CSS codes, Entanglement, Formal Verification, GHZ state, Mutation test-ing, Quantum Circuits, Quantum Error Correction, SMT, Soundness, Steane code, Surface code 

# 1 Introduction 

Quantum computing has the potential to outperform classical computing in several application domains, including communication, optimization, machine learning, and materials science [2–6]. Achieving this potential depends on two fundamental capabilities: the generation and preservation of quantum entanglement across multiple qubits, and the reliable detection and correction of errors 1arXiv:2610.03794v1 [quant-ph] 1 Oct 2026 arising from noise and decoherence in quantum hardware [7–10]. These capabilities are realized through quantum circuits whose correctness is essential for the reliability of larger quantum al-gorithms and protocols. Even a small implementation error, such as an omitted controlled-NOT (C-NOT) gate, an incorrectly placed Hadamard gate, or an improperly connected ancilla qubit, may alter the intended circuit behaviour. Such faults can invalidate entanglement generation or compromise error-detection mechanisms, thereby affecting the correctness of subsequent quantum computations. Verifying the functional correctness of quantum circuits remains challenging because quantum computation differs fundamentally from classical computation. The state space of an n-qubit system grows exponentially with the number of qubits, measurement outcomes are inherently probabilistic and disturb the quantum state, and arbitrary quantum states generally do not admit compact clas-sical representations. Formal verification techniques provide a systematic alternative to simulation by reasoning about all possible behaviours of a circuit rather than individual executions [11–13]. De-spite this advantage, the scalability of existing verification methods remains limited. Rewrite-rule-based verification using dyadic arithmetic has been demonstrated for circuits of up to 96 qubits, with scalability constrained by integer-overflow issues [14]. Decision-diagram-based property checking has been reported for circuits containing up to 10 qubits [15], while equivalence-checking approaches based on trusted reference circuits have been evaluated for circuits of up to 128 qubits, relying on Hilbert-space representations whenever binary-space reductions are not applicable [16, 17]. Our previous work [1] introduced an abstraction framework for a restricted but practically relevant class of quantum circuits composed solely of Hadamard (H), Pauli-X (X), and C-NOT gates. Within this gate set, the superposition status of each qubit and its computational-basis behaviour can be represented independently using two compact bit vectors. This representation transforms verification from reasoning over an exponentially large Hilbert space into a polynomial-sized satisfiability problem that can be solved using an off-the-shelf SMT solver. The approach was evaluated on Bell-state generation, GHZ-state generation, and the 2-qubit, 3-qubit, and Shor 9-qubit error-detection codes. In particular, GHZ-state circuits containing up to 8,192 qubits were verified in less than three minutes. Although the initial study demonstrated the feasibility of the abstraction, several important questions remained unanswered. First, it was unclear whether the abstraction extends beyond the specific code sizes evaluated previously or generalises to arbitrary circuit families. Second, its applicability to quantum error-correcting codes with more complex parity-check structures, such as CSS codes, had not been investigated. Third, the correctness lemmas underlying the abstraction were stated without complete proofs, and the class of circuits for which the abstractions remain faithful was not characterised. Moreover, the effectiveness of the properties in detecting implementation faults had not been systematically validated. Finally, the extent to which the reported scalability resulted from the abstraction itself, rather than from implementation-specific aspects of the SMT encoding or from the particular circuit structure, required further investigation. 2.3 Classical linear codes and the CSS construction 

The repetition codes considered in Section 2.2 are examples of a broader class of quantum error-correcting codes constructed from classical binary linear codes. The Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) construction was introduced by Calderbank and Shor [26] and, independently, by Steane [27] [28]. It is the only part of classical coding theory required in this work. A classical binary linear code C ⊆ { 0, 1}N with dimension Kc can be represented by a parity-check matrix H ∈ { 0, 1}r×N such that C = ker H over GF(2). If a codeword x ∈ C is affected by an error pattern e, the received word is x ⊕ e. Its syndrome is 

H(x ⊕ e) = He (mod 2) ,

because Hx = 0. Thus, the syndrome depends only on the error pattern. For example, a single error on bit j produces the jth column of H as its syndrome. If all columns of H are distinct and non-zero, as for the Hamming(7 , 4) code, each single-bit error has a different syndrome. The other codes considered in Section 6 include the [[15 , 7, 3]] quantum Hamming code [29], the [[15 , 1, 3]] quantum Reed-Muller code [30], for which HX̸ = HZ , and rotated surface codes [31, 32]. Not all stabilizer codes have the CSS form. For example, the five-qubit perfect code [33] contains stabilizer generators with Y -type factors and therefore cannot be represented by two separate binary parity-check matrices for the X and Z components. Such codes are naturally described using additive codes over GF(4), or equivalently as self-orthogonal subspaces of GF(2) 2N under the symplectic inner product [28, 34]. Capturing this additional algebraic structure would be necessary to extend the abstraction to general stabilizer codes, as discussed in Section 8. 

2.4 Error detection, identification, decoding, recovery, and fault tolerance 

The terms used for error handling can have different meanings, so we use the following defini-tions throughout the article. Error detection means that every error pattern in the specified class produces a non-zero syndrome. Error identification means that the measured syndrome uniquely determines the error pattern, up to multiplication by a stabilizer, within the considered class. De-coding is the classical procedure that maps a measured syndrome to a correction. Recovery is the subsequent application of this correction to the data qubits. Thus, in this article, error correction 

refers to the complete process of syndrome extraction, decoding, and recovery. 

Fault tolerance is a stronger requirement. It requires the syndrome-extraction and recovery cir-cuits themselves to tolerate faults, such as errors on ancilla qubits or C-NOT gates, without allowing these faults to spread into uncorrectable errors on the data qubits [7, 35]. The properties intro-duced in [1] establish correct syndrome extraction and identification for single errors. Section 4.5 extends these properties to arbitrary error patterns, detection up to a specified error weight, and decoding followed by recovery. Fault tolerance is not addressed by the present abstraction and remains outside its scope, as discussed in Section 7. 

2.5 Formal verification 

Formal verification establishes system correctness by proving that a design satisfies its specification over all possible behaviours rather than validating selected execution scenarios. It has been widely adopted for hardware and software systems [36–38]. Extending formal verification techniques to quantum circuits remains an active area of research because of the exponential growth of the quantum state space and the probabilistic nature of quantum measurement [12, 39]. The work presented in this article builds on the abstraction framework introduced in [1], which reduces verification of quantum circuits composed of Hadamard, Pauli-X, and C-NOT gates to a bit-vector satisfiability problem that can be solved using an SMT solver [40]. The following sections extend this framework to generalized circuit families and CSS quantum error-correcting codes, and evaluate its effectiveness through formal verification and mutation-based validation. 

# 3 Related Work 

Formal verification of quantum circuits has attracted increasing attention as quantum hardware and algorithms continue to advance. Existing verification techniques differ in their underlying 7representations and verification strategies, resulting in different trade-offs between expressiveness and scalability. The approaches most closely related to this work can be grouped into rewrite-rule-based verification, decision-diagram methods, equivalence checking, symbolic abstractions, and quantum program logics. Rewrite-rule-based verification has been investigated by Amy [14], who proposed a framework based on dyadic arithmetic and rewrite rules implemented within a Haskell theorem prover. The method supports verification of universal quantum circuits but relies on dyadic representations whose arithmetic becomes increasingly difficult to manage as circuit size grows. Reported experi-ments demonstrate verification for circuits containing up to 96 qubits, with larger instances limited by integer-overflow issues. Decision-diagram representations have also been explored for quantum circuit verification. Seiter et al. [15] employed multiple-valued decision diagrams to verify properties of quantum circuits, including implementations of Grover’s algorithm. Although decision diagrams provide compact representations for many classes of circuits, their effectiveness decreases as the amount of entanglement increases, and the reported evaluation is limited to circuits containing up to 10 qubits. A different line of research focuses on equivalence checking, in which the implementation under verification is compared with a trusted reference circuit. Burgholzer and Wille [16] and Yamashita and Markov [17] developed equivalence-checking techniques for quantum circuits using this strategy. These approaches assume the availability of a correct reference implementation and rely on Hilbert-space representations when binary-space reductions cannot be applied. Experimental evaluations reported in these studies include circuits containing up to 128 qubits. Symbolic abstractions provide an alternative by reducing quantum-circuit verification to rea-soning over compact symbolic representations. Our earlier work introduced symbolic, rotational, and superposition-based abstractions for quantum circuits represented as bit vectors [41–44]. These abstractions were applied to the verification of the Quantum Fourier Transform, its inverse, quan-tum data-encoding circuits, and quantum phase-estimation circuits. The abstraction framework presented in [1] extended this approach to entanglement-generation circuits and quantum error-detection circuits composed of Hadamard, Pauli-X, and C-NOT gates. That study demonstrated the feasibility of the abstraction for Bell-state generation, GHZ-state generation, and the 2-qubit, 3-qubit, and Shor 9-qubit error-detection codes, but it considered only fixed code sizes and presented the underlying correctness lemmas without formal proofs. A closely related, but different, line of work concerns the efficient simulation of stabilizer circuits. The Gottesman-Knill theorem [45] shows that circuits composed of Clifford gates, including H,

X, and C-NOT, together with computational-basis measurements, can be simulated classically in polynomial time. The tableau method of Aaronson and Gottesman [46] requires O(n2) bits for n

qubits, with O(n) time per gate and O(n2) time per measurement. Stim [47] provides a highly optimised implementation that is widely used in quantum error-correction studies. Simulation and the SMT-based verification considered in this article serve different purposes. A simulator evaluates the behaviour of a circuit for a particular input, or samples its behaviour under sampled noise. In contrast, an SMT query checks whether a property holds for all admissible inputs and error patterns, or produces a counterexample when it does not. Error patterns are represented symbolically, so a single query can cover, for example, all 2 N error patterns considered by Property 8. The scalability results in Section 6 should therefore not be interpreted as showing that the proposed abstraction is more efficient than stabilizer simulation. Stabilizer simulation applies in polynomial time to the full Clifford group, whereas the abstraction considered here is limited to the fragment F defined in Definition 12. Instead, the results show that the universally quantified verification queries generated by the abstraction remain tractable for the circuit families evaluated in this study. 8Formal reasoning about quantum programs has also been studied from the perspective of pro-gram logics. Liu et al. [11] proposed a quantum Hoare logic for verifying the correctness of quantum algorithms, including amplitude-amplification procedures. This line of work is complementary to circuit-level functional verification, as it focuses on proving algorithmic correctness rather than ver-ifying the structural correctness of specific quantum circuit implementations. Broader surveys of formal verification techniques for quantum programs and circuits have been presented by Chareton 

et al. [12], Quist et al. [39], and Govindankutty [13], highlighting the diversity of existing approaches and the continuing challenges associated with scalable verification. The work in this article directly extends the abstraction framework introduced in [1]. Table 1 in Section 1 gives a detailed comparison of the material carried over from [1] and the new contributions. The present work extends the verification methodology to arbitrary repetition-code families and to CSS codes defined by arbitrary parity-check matrices. It also defines the circuit fragment for which the abstraction is sound and provides a proof of soundness, together with a systematic evaluation based on mutation-based fault injection. Mutation testing has been used for many years to evaluate verification and testing techniques in software and hardware systems, following the original work of DeMillo, Lipton, and Sayward [48]; a survey is given in [49]. One challenge in mutation testing is the equivalent-mutant problem: a mutant may be functionally identical to the original system and therefore cannot be distinguished from it by any test. In general, determining equivalence is undecidable [49, 50]. For the finite-state abstract circuits considered in this article, however, equivalence is decidable. We therefore identify equivalent mutants automatically using an SMT miter, as described in Section 5.2.

4.1 Base abstraction 

The proposed verification framework represents quantum circuits using a compact bit-vector ab-straction. Each qubit is modelled by two bit vectors that capture the information required for reasoning about superposition and computational-basis behaviour under the restricted gate set considered in this work.

The correctness properties introduced above are defined entirely in terms of the proposed bit-vector abstraction. Definition 12 (Fragment F). Consider a circuit over {H, X, C-NOT } with computational-basis inputs, where phase-flip errors may also be injected. Branch qubits receive exactly one Hadamard gate, and this gate is applied before the qubit participates in any C-NOT gate. Frame qubits receive an even number of Hadamard gates arranged in consecutive pairs. q0. Outside these frame segments, the qubit may participate in arbitrary X and C-NOT gates. Measurements (Definition 1) and classically controlled X gates (Definition 11) are applied only to qubits with an even tag, and only after all gates acting on those measured qubits have been completed. Membership in F is a syntactic condition and can be checked by scanning the gate list once. All circuits evaluated in this article belong to F. The bit-flip repetition and CSS circuits use only computational qubits. The concatenated and CSS phase-flip circuits use frame qubits with one frame segment. The Bell and GHZ circuits use one branch qubit. The random networks in Section 6.7 use both branch and computational qubits.

# 5 Verification Methodology 

This section describes the SMT-based verification workflow, the size of the generated constraint system, the mutation-based validation strategy used to evaluate the proposed properties, and the experimental environment in which all evaluations were conducted. 

5.1 SMT encoding and verification 

Each circuit family introduced in Section 4 is encoded directly using the Z3 Python API [40]. This avoids the intermediate SMT-LIB representation used in our earlier implementation. Each abstract qubit is represented by two fixed-width BitVec terms for its ( s, q ) components. The abstract gates defined in Definitions 2-4 are encoded as equality constraints between fresh output bit-vectors and the corresponding input bit-vectors. Conditional behaviour is represented using Z3’s If expressions. Constant inputs, such as ancillas initialised to b′00, are represented by bit-vector literals. Similarly, the tags of qubits that do not receive a Hadamard gate remain literals throughout the encoding. Faults and error locations are represented by free 1-bit variables. For properties that restrict the number of errors, these variables are constrained by a cardinality condition P 

> i

ei ≤ w, encoded using Z3’s native pseudo-Boolean constraint AtMost . Code-word preconditions ( HZ v = 0 or HX v =0) are encoded as XOR constraints over the free data variables. Given a correctness property P ,verification asserts the circuit constraints together with its negation, ¬P , and invokes the solver using check() . An unsat result shows that the property holds for all valid assignments of the symbolic variables. For circuits in F, Corollary 1 then transfers this result to the corresponding concrete circuit. Conversely, a sat result provides a counterexample, namely an assignment that violates the property. 

Size of the constraint system. Consider a circuit with n qubits, gH Hadamard gates, gX Xgates or error injections, and gC C-NOT gates. The encoding introduces one fresh 3-bit variable for each Hadamard gate and one fresh 2-bit variable for each X gate, error injection, C-NOT gate and measurement, together with one equality constraint for each. Thus, the number of SMT variables and assertions is O(n + gH + gX + gC ). It is independent of the circuit depth except through 21 the number of gates. Each constraint is a fixed-width If /XOR relation involving at most three bit-vectors of width at most three. After bit-blasting, this gives a constant number of clauses per gate, so the propositional encoding also has O(n + gH + gX + gC ) clauses. The property itself is the main source of non-constant-size constraints, with one conjunct for each checked qubit. The cardinality constraint over the N error variables is handled by Z3’s pseudo-Boolean reasoning; for the clause counts reported below, the card2bv tactic compiles this constraint into O(N log N ) clauses. The code-word preconditions contribute O(nnz( H)) XOR terms. For a GHZ circuit with m qubits, the encoding therefore contains m + 1 variables and m gate constraints for all three topologies. For a CSS circuit, the size is O(N + nnz( H)). Section 6.6 reports the measured numbers of variables, assertions and CNF clauses after Z3’s 

simplify , card2bv , bit-blast and tseitin-cnf tactics. These measurements show the expected linear growth of the encoding. Linear encoding size, however, does not imply linear solving time. In general, deciding the validity of such formulas is co-NP-complete, and solver performance depends on the structure of the constraints. Section 6.7 shows that dense XOR dependencies, such as those produced by deep random C-NOT networks, are considerably harder to solve than the sparse dependencies in the code and GHZ circuits. Cardinality constraints provide a second source of difficulty. For large surface codes, the single-error query is slower than the arbitrary-error query of Property 8, even though the latter implies it. Decoders that compare error weights also make recovery queries more expensive (Section 6.4). 

5.2 Mutation-based fault injection 

To complement the formal proofs in Section 4.7, we evaluate the fault-detection capability of the proposed framework using mutation testing. Each circuit-construction routine accepts an optional mutation operator and its target location, allowing faulty circuit variants to be generated automat-ically. The operators, their target locations and the circuit families to which they apply are listed in Table 3. They cover the fault classes considered in Lemmas 1-5, including missing, additional and incorrectly connected gates, as well as incorrect ancilla initialisation. Each operator is applied at every valid location in every circuit instance. The resulting distri-bution of mutants is therefore determined by the circuit structure rather than selected manually. This avoids selection bias and makes the complete sweep exactly reproducible. 

Equivalent and non-generated mutants. A mutant that produces exactly the same abstract outputs as the original circuit for every admissible input cannot be detected by any functional property. Following standard mutation-testing practice [48, 49], such mutants are excluded. Rather than identifying them by inspection, we determine equivalence mechanically using an SMT miter .The original circuit and the mutant are constructed using the same input and error variables but separate internal variables, together with the admissibility constraints. The solver then searches for an assignment for which any output component ( s, q ) of any qubit differs between the two circuits. An unsat result proves equivalence, whereas a sat result proves non-equivalence. Since the ab-stract circuits are finite-state, this procedure is complete, so no equivalent mutant remains uniden-tified among the evaluated mutants. Only mutants proved to be non-equivalent by the miter are evaluated against the correctness property. If a mutation location does not exist, the mutant is reported as not generated rather than silently replacing it with the unmutated circuit. For example, this occurs when the C-NOT of the last GHZ qubit is re-targeted to a non-existent next qubit. We report the median over three processes, together with the baseline memory usage of the interpreter with Z3 loaded. Unlike the preliminary study [1], which used a 24-core workstation with 192 GB of memory, the experiments reported here were conducted on a commodity laptop. This configuration was chosen to assess the practicality of the proposed abstraction under hardware resources representative of a typical research and software-development environment.

6.7 Random H+C-NOT networks 

GHZ, repetition and surface-code circuits produce sparse XOR relations, where each output depends on only a small number of variables. To examine less favourable constraint structures, we also verified random networks. Each network applies Hadamard gates to a random half of the qubits as the first gate (branch qubits), followed by D layers of C-NOT gates acting on random disjoint qubit pairs with random orientations, together with sparse X gates. The property requires each output basis bit to equal the GF(2) affine function obtained inde-pendently from the gate list, and each tag to be b′001 exactly for the Hadamard qubits. As D

increases, the number of input variables on which an output depends, referred to as its support, grows rapidly. The results are shown in Table 11 and Figure 4. For shallow networks, verification remains fast even for thousands of qubits. At a fixed width, however, solve time increases rapidly with depth. For 32 qubits, the median solve time increases from 0.033s at depth 4 to 27s at depth 7, while at depth 8 two of the three seeds exceed the 300s timeout. For 64 qubits, the median increases from 0.20s at depth 5 to 125s at depth 7. The circuit size is small in all of these cases. The experiments show that the proposed abstraction verifies all benchmark circuits considered in this study while remaining computationally efficient for circuits with sparse XOR structure. The generalised repetition and concatenated phase-flip experiments show that the correctness properties extend to parametrised circuit families. The CSS experiments show that the abstraction can be used for syndrome extraction with arbitrary CSS check matrices. The multiple-error experiments distinguish detection, identification and recovery and confirm the expected limits of the codes considered. The GHZ experiments show that the implementation scales to circuits with more than 2 .6 × 10 5

qubits for all three evaluated topologies under the proposed abstraction. The random-network and recovery experiments also show the limits of this scaling. The differential test provides an independent check of the soundness theorem on its stated fragment and shows its failure outside that fragment. The mutation study provides empirical evidence that the implemented correctness properties detect the fault classes considered in Section 4.7. Taken together, the results show that the proposed verification framework can handle the circuit classes studied here at substantial scale, while also identifying constraint structures and decoder properties that lead to higher verification costs. 

# 7 Discussion: Scope, Limitations, and Threats to Validity 

This section discusses the scope of the proposed abstraction, its current limitations, and the main threats to the validity of the experimental evaluation. 

7.1 Scope of the abstraction 

The abstraction introduced in Section 4, together with the soundness result in Theorem 1, applies to circuits in the fragment F defined in Definition 12. This fragment is a proper subset of the circuits composed of H, X and C-NOT gates, and therefore the method is not a verification technique for general stabilizer circuits. The fragment is sufficient for all circuit families studied in this work, including repetition and concatenated codes, Bell and GHZ state preparation, random H+C-NOT networks, and CSS syn-drome extraction. This includes CSS codes with HX̸ = HZ , such as the [[15 , 1, 3]] quantum Reed-Muller code and the surface codes. Within these circuits, phase-flip errors are represented using the frame interpretation described in Section 4.1, rather than by an explicit Z gate. A physical bit-flip fault occurring inside a Hadamard frame segment is therefore outside the model. As shown by counterexample C1 in Remark 1, the abstraction would interpret such a fault as a phase flip. The abstraction also does not directly cover stabilizer codes whose generators contain intrinsic 

Y -type Pauli operators that cannot be represented as purely X-type or purely Z-type operators on the same support. A representative example is the five-qubit perfect code [33], whose generators 

XZZXI and their cyclic shifts contain both X and Z factors on overlapping qubit sets, so that products of generators can contain Y factors. Its stabilizer group therefore cannot be generated entirely from separate X-type and Z-type elements and has no CSS decomposition [34]. Supporting such codes would require additional gate semantics, in particular an abstract phase ( S) gate, together with a representation of the relative phases ω in Eq. (7), rather than only the current superposition status (Section 8). 40 Circuit-level verification versus physical validation. The framework verifies logical proper-ties of an abstract circuit description. These include which syndrome is computed, whether gates are connected as intended, and whether the expected entanglement structure is produced. It does not model hardware noise, decoherence, leakage, measurement errors, gate infidelities, crosstalk, timing effects, or other physical-device behaviour. In this work, the terms fault and mutant refer to design or implementation errors in the circuit description, whereas errors denote the discrete Pauli bit-flip and phase-flip patterns that the code is intended to handle. Establishing that a physical device correctly implements the verified circuit, or determining its logical error rate under a realistic noise model, requires complementary methods such as noise-aware simulation [47] and experimental characterisation. 

7.2 Detection, correction, and fault tolerance 

Section 2.4 distinguishes detection, identification, decoding, recovery and fault tolerance. The properties of [1] and Properties 4-7 verify correct syndrome extraction and error identification under a single error. Property 8 extends syndrome verification to arbitrary error patterns, while Property 9 verifies detection up to a specified error weight. Property 10 additionally verifies that a specified decoder, together with classically controlled corrections, returns the data to the error-free code word for all errors up to weight w. For the generalised repetition code with n > 3, the single-error properties establish identification only. Correction of multiple errors is established by Property 10 for the values of n and w that were verified in Section 6.4. These properties do not establish fault tolerance . In all verified circuits, the error variables act on the data qubits before syndrome extraction, while the extraction circuit itself is assumed to be fault-free. Faults affecting ancillas or C-NOT gates during extraction are not modelled, and neither is their propagation. In particular, a phase fault on an ancilla that is the target of several C-NOT gates can propagate to the control (data) qubits through phase kickback. Similarly, an ancilla prepared in |+⟩ that controls several C-NOT gates can propagate an X fault to subsequent targets. Either mechanism can convert a single fault into a correlated multi-qubit data error, commonly referred to as a hook error [52]. The present abstraction cannot represent these mechanisms. Phase kickback is a phase effect that is not captured by Definition 4. The second mechanism requires a qubit that has acted as a C-NOT control while in superposition to receive a second Hadamard gate, which is excluded from F, as shown by counterexample C2. Consequently, for the Steane code, the framework verifies the classical Hamming(7 , 4) syndrome logic of the ancilla-based extraction circuit in both Pauli frames. It does not verify the fault tolerance of the complete extraction circuit. Such verification would require, for example, modelling verified ancilla preparation, repeated extraction or flag qubits [7, 35]. More generally, fault-tolerance verification would require error variables at each location in the extraction circuit and an error-propagation semantics that includes phase kickback, as outlined in the phase-aware extension in Section 8. 

7.3 Error model and preconditions 

The single-error properties assume that at most one error is present. For the Steane and CSS properties, they also assume that the input is a valid code word, namely HZ v = 0 or HX v = 0, respectively. This code-word precondition is part of the specification of syndrome extraction: the syndrome of a non-code word is not an error syndrome. Producing a valid code word is therefore the responsibility of the encoding circuit, which can be verified separately and then composed with 41 the extraction circuit. The single-error assumption, in contrast, is not intrinsic to syndrome extraction. Property 8 removes this restriction and verifies syndrome extraction for every error pattern. Properties 9 and 10 then specify the error weights up to which detection and recovery are required to hold. The current model does not include errors that occur during syndrome extraction or recovery (Section 7.2), measurement errors, or repeated rounds of syndrome extraction. 

7.4 Coverage of the mutation analysis 

The mutation study in Section 6.10 focuses on implementation faults directly covered by Lem-mas 1-6. These include incorrect ancilla initialisation, omitted gates, additional gates and incorrect C-NOT wiring. Each mutant contains a single injected modification. Although combinations of modifications are covered by the characterisation in Lemma 6, they were not generated systemati-cally in the mutation experiment. Other fault classes were not considered. These include the insertion of gates outside the sup-ported {H, X, C-NOT } set, such as Z or S gates, timing-related faults in physical implementations, and systematically generated combinations of multiple simultaneous faults. The reported 100% detection rate should therefore be interpreted narrowly. It means that every non-equivalent single-modification mutant from the listed operator classes, for the listed circuit instances, violated its corresponding property. It is not evidence of complete fault coverage or of detection of physically realistic fault classes. Its main purpose is to show that the implemented encodings behave as predicted by Lemmas 1-6, as discussed in Section 5.2. 

7.5 Interpretation of the scalability results 

The GHZ-state experiments show substantially improved verification performance compared with the implementation reported in [1]. This comparison requires some care for three reasons. First, the GHZ, repetition, concatenated and surface-code queries contain sparse linear XOR relationships in which each output depends on only a small number of variables. These structures are well suited to SMT solving, and their solve times grow approximately linearly over the measured range. The reported scale is therefore a property of these circuit families under the proposed abstraction. It should not be interpreted as evidence that arbitrary circuits with 2 .6 × 10 5 qubits can be verified. The random H+C-NOT experiments in Section 6.7 show the other side of this result. When outputs depend on many input variables, proving equality between the XOR structure of the circuit and that of the specification becomes substantially harder. At fixed width, solve times therefore increase rapidly with circuit depth. This behaviour is consistent with the known difficulty of parity reasoning for resolution-based solvers [51]. Solvers with native XOR reasoning [53], or approaches based on Gaussian elimination, would be natural alternatives for these constraints because the underlying parity problems are decidable in polynomial time by linear algebra. Second, the reported scale should be considered in the context of stabilizer simulation [46, 47]. GHZ preparation is a Clifford circuit and can be simulated in polynomial time using tableau-based methods. The SMT queries address a different task: they verify a property for all admissible inputs and error patterns simultaneously, or return a counterexample. The results therefore measure the ability of the solver to handle the linear bit-vector constraints produced by the abstraction, rather than general symbolic reasoning over high-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Third, the comparison with [1] is not a controlled experiment because the original implementa-tion was not re-executed under identical conditions and the hardware differs. Section 6.8 therefore 42 isolates the factors that could be controlled. On the same hardware and for identical constraints, it compares the direct API pipeline with an SMT-LIB serialisation pipeline and also compares two Z3 releases. Neither factor explains the observed improvement. In particular, the stand-alone solver is faster on the SMT-LIB file than solving the API-built query, and the two Z3 releases have similar performance. The remaining differences, including the hardware and the details of the con-straint generator used in [1], could not be isolated. The comparison with [1] should consequently be regarded as observational rather than controlled. Finally, beyond 262,144 qubits, the main resource limitation is Python-side construction and memory rather than solver time (Section 6.5). At the largest size, construction of the Python term graph takes about five times longer than solving, 46s compared with 9s. Peak memory is also reached during construction and grows approximately linearly at around 5.3KiB per qubit. On the 16 GB machine used in the experiments, this corresponds to a limit of approximately three million qubits before the available memory is exhausted. 

7.6 Threats to validity of the soundness argument 

Theorem 1 is proved for the fragment F, and every benchmark circuit used in the experiments belongs to this fragment by construction. The theorem does not impose a bound on the number of Hadamard gates applied to a qubit. A frame qubit may pass through any number of frame segments, corresponding to repeated encode-decode cycles, because the saturating tag preserves parity (Definition 2). The theorem does require that, between two Hadamard gates, a qubit participates in no gate other than phase-flip error injections. It also requires that a qubit which has interacted with another qubit does not receive a further Hadamard gate. The need for these conditions is illustrated by counterexamples C1-C4. The differential test in Section 6.9 further supports the theorem, including tests with random circuits containing up to eight frame segments per qubit and therefore exercising tag saturation. Circuits outside F, including circuits that apply Hadamard gates to entangled qubits, circuits containing S or Z gates, or circuits in which data qubits in superposition act as C-NOT controls between Hadamard gates, require a richer abstract state. These cases are left for future work. 

7.7 Applicability to CSS codes 

The Steane experiment demonstrated the feasibility of the approach for one CSS construction in which the same Hamming matrix defines both check types. The evaluation extends this to a code with HX = HZ and higher check weight, namely the [[15 , 7, 3]] code; a code with HX̸ = HZ and asymmetric distances, namely the [[15 , 1, 3]] quantum Reed-Muller code with dX = 7 and dZ = 3; and a family with a two-dimensional local check structure and degenerate syndromes, namely the rotated surface codes. All of these cases are handled by Definition 10 without modifying the underlying gate abstrac-tion. This supports the use of the framework for CSS syndrome extraction because the corre-sponding property is defined by the pair ( HX , HZ ). The scope of this result is limited to syndrome extraction, detection, and look-up or minimum-weight recovery. It does not extend to fault-tolerant implementations or to decoders for degenerate codes, such as minimum-weight matching for surface codes, whose verification is left for future work. 43 8 Conclusion and Future Directions 

This paper extends the bit-vector abstraction proposed in [1] for the formal verification of quantum error-detection and entanglement circuits in several directions. First, the original correctness prop-erties are generalised from fixed-size examples to parametrised repetition and concatenated code families. Second, the abstraction is applied to the Steane [[7 , 1, 3]] CSS code, demonstrating that the framework is applicable to a representative quantum error-correcting code beyond repetition-based constructions. Third, a soundness theorem is established for the abstraction within its supported gate set, and complete proofs are provided for the six lemmas that were stated without proof in the original work. Fourth, these theoretical results are supported by a mutation-based evaluation comprising 120 circuit mutants, all of which were successfully detected by the proposed verification properties. Finally, the verification framework is reimplemented using a direct Z3 API encoding, enabling GHZ-state verification at substantially larger scales than previously reported while using considerably fewer computational resources. Taken together, these results indicate that the proposed abstraction is not limited to the fixed-size examples considered in the original study. The generalised correctness properties, the verifica-tion of the Steane code, and the accompanying theoretical and empirical analyses demonstrate that the framework can be applied to a broader class of quantum circuits while maintaining efficient SMT-based verification. Several directions remain for future research. The first is to extend the abstraction beyond the current {H, X, C-NOT } gate set to support stabilizer codes containing genuine Y -type operators, such as the five-qubit perfect code. This will require enriching the abstract state representation to capture relative phase information. A second direction is to move beyond the current single-error assumption by modelling multiple simultaneous errors together with the corresponding recovery operations, thereby enabling end-to-end verification of complete fault-tolerant protocols. Finally, the mutation-based evaluation framework presented in this work could be extended and adopted more broadly as a complementary validation methodology for quantum circuit verification studies, providing empirical evidence for the fault-detection capability of proposed correctness properties. Overall, the experimental results demonstrate that the proposed SMT-based verification frame-work remains computationally efficient across the circuit families investigated, while the theoretical analysis establishes the correctness guarantees of the abstraction within its intended scope. These findings further support the use of SMT solving as a practical approach for the formal verification of quantum circuits constructed from the supported gate set.
