# Bit-Precise CHC Satisfiability Using Theory-Modular Reasoning 

Omer Rappoport , Orna Grumberg , and Yakir Vizel 

Technion - Israel Institute of Technology, Haifa, Israel 

{omer.r,orna,yvizel}@cs.technion.ac.il 

Abstract. Deciding satisfiability of Constrained Horn Clauses (CHCs) modulo the theory of fixed-size bit-vectors ( TB) is fundamental to bit-precise program verification. However, state-of-the-art CHC-solvers often struggle with TB, limiting scalability in bit-precise reasoning. We present Mosaic , a theory-modular framework for deciding satisfia-bility of CHCs modulo TB by combining reasoning in TB and the theory of Integer Arithmetic ( TI). Given a CHC set modulo TB, Mosaic par-titions it into two fragments interpreted over TB and TI. Moreover, it implements an algorithm that reasons about the fragments in a modu-lar fashion, exchanges information between them via sound translations across theories, and determines satisfiability w.r.t. the original CHC set. We implemented a prototype of Mosaic using Z3 and Spacer and eval-uated it on bit-manipulating benchmarks. Our evaluation shows that 

Mosaic significantly outperforms Spacer on these benchmarks. 

## 1 Introduction 

Constrained Horn Clauses (CHCs) is a fragment of First Order Logic (FOL) with many practical applications in Formal Methods [3,17,9]. In particular, program safety can be reduced to satisfiability of a set of CHCs. Often, the reduction of program safety to CHCs is done w.r.t. the theory of Integer Arithmetic ( TI). Since CHC-solvers perform well when Integer Arithmetic is used as a background theory, this results in efficient program safety verification. However, when pro-gram operations are required to be represented soundly relative to the low-level bit representation of data, CHC-solvers do not perform as efficiently. Encoding program safety verification w.r.t. bit-precise semantics can be nat-urally achieved through a reduction to CHCs modulo the theory of Fixed-Size Bit-Vectors ( TB). Alternatively, bit-precision can be modeled using TI despite the fact that the domain is infinite. This is done by representing bit-precise op-erations and modular arithmetic via multiplication, division, and modulo con-straints [19] 1. However, neither approach consistently yields an efficient verifica-tion procedure. This encoding choice presents a fundamental trade-off: TB for-mulations often hinder the generalization capabilities of CHC-solvers, whereas bit-precise TI encodings produce complex constraints that are computationally expensive to process, particularly in the presence of bit-wise operations.

In this paper, we present Mosaic , a novel framework designed to efficiently decide the satisfiability of CHCs modulo TB by leveraging the complementary strengths of both TB and TI. Given a set of CHCs Π(TB), Mosaic first parti-tions the input into two distinct fragments, (ΠB(TB), Π I(TI)) , each interpreted under its respective background theory. Mosaic then employs a dual-reasoning algorithm that processes both sets in a modular fashion to determine the sat-isfiability of the original Π(TB). The core intuition behind Mosaic is to exploit the fact that CHC-solvers handle CHCs modulo TI effectively when limited to arithmetic operations. By isolating purely arithmetic constraints in ΠI(TI) while retaining bit-wise operations in ΠB(TB), Mosaic bridges the aforementioned gap, effectively “enjoying the best of both worlds” to achieve superior performance. Deciding the satisfiability of Π(TB) using the decomposed sets (ΠB(TB), Π I(TI)) 

is a non-trivial task that requires bridging the differences in signatures and se-mantics of TB and TI through sound transformations. To enable modular rea-soning, Mosaic integrates three essential components: (i) a theory transformer 

to translate formulas and structures between TB and TI; (ii) an interface to facilitate communication of reasoning steps between ΠB and ΠI; and (iii) an al-gorithm that leverages these components to analyze both fragments and decide the satisfiability of the original Π(TB) while ensuring soundness. Decomposing Π(TB) into (ΠB, Π I) is done in three steps. First, the set Π is partitioned into two distinct subsets (ΠB, Π I) such that their union is Π. While the partitioning can be done arbitrarily, in our prototype implementation it is performed according to the constraints that appear in the body of CHCs in 

Π. Specifically, CHCs that include only arithmetic operations are placed in ΠI,while CHCs that include bit-wise operations are placed in ΠB. Next, ΠI is re-encoded w.r.t. TI using a theory transformer, which translates constraints over TB

to constraints over TI. Lastly, ΠB and ΠI share uninterpreted predicate symbols. For every shared predicate symbol p, two copies, pB and pI, are created. Then, 

p is substituted with pB and pI in ΠB and ΠI, respectively. If p is an interface predicate , namely, w.l.o.g. p appears in the head of a clause in ΠB and in the body of a clause in ΠI, it is essential to maintain the “logical connection” between ΠB

and ΠI. To achieve that, a set of interface constraints Θ is generated for all such interface predicates. These constraints ensure consistency w.r.t. interpretations of the newly created copies of p. The result of these steps is the tuple ΠB|I =(ΠB, Π I, Θ ), called a theory-modular encoding .Reasoning over a theory-modular encoding ΠB|I = ( ΠB, Π I, Θ ) is performed by MosaicSolve . The procedure relies on existing CHC-solvers for TB and TI.It generates single-theory queries, delegates them to the corresponding CHC-solvers, and coordinates the exchange of information between fragments as dic-tated by the interface constraints. We implemented a proof-of-concept of Mosaic using Z3 [12] and its CHC-solver Spacer [11]. For the evaluation, we created a benchmark with several examples that highlight the benefits of Mosaic over a standard CHC-solver. 1.1 Related Work 

To the best of our knowledge, prior work on CHC-SAT has not explored a theory-modular approach that combines multiple background theories within a single framework. The work in [16] partitions a set of CHCs based on its structure, but all subsets are interpreted over the same background theory. In contrast, Mosaic 

enables coordinated reasoning across different theories. In program verification, several works combine reasoning over TB and TI. For example, [8] alternates between reasoning in TI and validating in TB, while other approaches [18,5] rely on abstraction and refinement. In contrast, Mosaic does not use abstraction and captures the semantics of TB precisely. Transforming logical formulas between theories, both syntactically and se-mantically, is not a new concept. In Institution theory [6,7], such transforma-tions are formalized via signature and institution morphisms. In the context of Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT), similar translations have been devel-oped, in particular for TB [14,19,2]. Theory combination in SMT is classically handled by the Nelson-Oppen framework [13], which assumes stable infiniteness and thus does not apply to TB, and restricts communication to equalities over shared variables. More general frameworks based on polite theories [15,10] can handle TB. In contrast, Mosaic operates in the CHC-SAT setting and, rather than combining theories within a single formula, decomposes CHCs into frag-ments interpreted over different theories and coordinates them via the exchange of general quantifier-free formulas, going beyond equality-based communication.

## 3 Theory Transformers

The first transformer, denoted τB⇝I, translates bit-vector formulas into integer arithmetic, while the second transformer, denoted τI⇝B, translates formulas in the opposite direction. Π, each fragment is reasoned about in its respective theory, and the transformers 

τB⇝I and τI⇝B enable the transfer of information between fragments. 4.1 The Encoding 

We now introduce our theory-modular encoding of Π. The encoding is defined with respect to a partition of Π into two fragments. The first fragment, the 

bit-vector fragment , consists of CHCs reasoned about with respect to TB. These CHCs are left unchanged, except that each uninterpreted predicate p is replaced by a fresh copy pB. p is replaced by a fresh copy pI, and variables are consistently renamed according to the variable mapping. ## 5 Algorithm 

This section presents MosaicSolve , a symbolic procedure for deciding the sat-isfiability of theory-modular encodings. The procedure relies on two CHC sat-isfiability oracles: one for the bit-vector fragment modulo TB and one for the integer fragment modulo TI. At a high level, MosaicSolve generates queries over a single theory and delegates them to the corresponding oracles, while co-ordinating the exchange of information between fragments. This coordination is achieved by propagating constraints soundly across interface constraints using theory transformers, and by maintaining both under- and over-approximations of the external semantics of uninterpreted predicates. The under-approximations are used to search for counterexamples to satisfiability, i.e., derivations leading to a query violation, while the over-approximations are used to block infeasible counterexamples and detect convergence. ## 6 Implementation Details and Experimental Results 

This section describes the implementation of the Mosaic framework and its eval-uation. We present the theory transformers used in our implementation, the strategy for partitioning a CHC set into theory fragments, and an optimization applied during the encoding. Our prototype is implemented using Z3 [12] and 

Spacer [11], where Spacer serves as the CHC-SAT oracle for both TB and TI.

Theory Transformers. Recall that translations are defined with respect to domain transformers (Definition 5), which map elements in the domain of each bit-vector variable to elements in the domain of its corresponding integer vari-able. Two natural choices are signed and unsigned semantics. In programming languages, signedness is typically part of a variable’s type (e.g., int vs. unsigned int in C). In contrast, in ΣB, the sort of a variable depends only on its bit-width, while signedness is reflected in certain predicates and functions (e.g., <B 

> u

vs. <B 

> s

). Consequently, the same variable may appear in both signed and unsigned con-texts. We adopt the following strategy. For each bit-vector variable, if it occurs more frequently in unsigned than signed predicate or function applications in Π,we use the unsigned domain transformer. Otherwise, we use the signed one. We now describe the formula transformers τB⇝I and τI⇝B (Definition 7). For 

τB⇝I, we use the translation of Zohar et al. [19], which maps QF_BV formulas to 

QF_IA formulas and satisfies Eq. 2. Since the translation in [19] assumes unsigned semantics, we extend it to signed semantics by adjusting domain restrictions and inserting the appropriate signed-unsigned conversions. ## 7 Conclusion 

We presented Mosaic , a theory-modular framework for deciding satisfiability of CHCs modulo TB. It reduces CHCs into two fragments interpreted over TB and TI,connected by interface constraints. We introduced a procedure for determining satisfiability of such encodings and implemented a prototype. Our evaluation shows that the approach can significantly outperform state-of-the-art solvers.
