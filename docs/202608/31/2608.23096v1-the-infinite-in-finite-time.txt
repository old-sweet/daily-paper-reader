Abstract 

Linear-time temporal properties, such as those described by Linear-time Temporal Logic, are typically modelled as sets of infinite traces. Yet, in a run-time verification context, such as when testing or monitoring a system, only a finite prefix of the system’s behaviour can be observed. For some properties, these finite prefixes may be definitive —a yes or no answer can be given without further observation. By enriching the semantics of LTL with these definitive prefixes, we give a proper inductive accounting of the semantics of LTL 3, a multi-valued variant of Linear-time Temporal Logic for run-time verification applications. The semantic descriptions of LTL 3 in pre-vious work are given only in terms of their relationship to conventional LTL. We show that the semantics of LTL and of LTL 3 are isomorphic. In addition, we formalise the formula progression evaluation technique, popularly used in runtime verification contexts, and show its soundness and completeness up to finite traces with respect to our semantics. Then, we turn to linear-time properties more generally: using our theory of definitive prefixes, we re-prove the well-known safety-liveness decomposition theorem, and reconstruct the topology of infinite traces. We define monitorability for properties, providing neat topological characterisations for various monitorability classes, and ar-range them into a hierarchy. All of our definitions and proofs are mechanised in Isabelle/HOL. 

Keywords: Linear-time Temporal Logic, Partial Traces, Semantics, Formula Progression, Safety Properties, Liveness Properties, Guarantee Properties, Topology, Monitorability

In the specification, verification and modelling of reactive and concurrent systems, linear-time properties are a recurring theme. Linear-time proper-ties are sets of behaviours : completed, infinite traces —sequences of states— describing the execution of a system over time. Logics for the specification of such properties, such as Linear-time Temporal Logic (LTL) [1] are well-studied and widely used, and a variety of algorithmic techniques for model checking and verification exist [2]. In the context of run-time verification, testing, or monitoring, however, semantics in terms of behaviours appears insufficient, as the full infinite behaviour of the system cannot be observed: we can only observe a finite sequence of states. In light of this, we might consider switching to a semantics which includes finite traces as models of temporal properties. There are several variants of LTL which include finite traces as models, the oldest of which, called LTL f ,is commonly attributed to Pnueli 1. These logics concern finite or infinite 

completed traces, but this is also not suitable in the context of run-time verification, as our finite observations are not completed traces, but finite 

prefixes of infinite behaviours. In other words, the behaviour of the system is still infinite, it is only our observation of the behaviour that is finite. Therefore, in this paper we advocate remaining with infinite trace se-mantics even in a run-time verification context. If we are monitoring for the property “eventually, a good state is reached”, then our observation can stop the moment a good state is reached, as the property has been satisfied already and no further observation is needed. Similarly, if we a monitoring for the property “the bad state is never reached”, our observation can stop as soon as the bad state is reached, as the property has been irrevocably vio-lated. Thus, there are properties for which definitive answers—satisfaction or violation—can be determined after observation of just a finite prefix. We call such prefixes definitive prefixes , and sets of infinite traces and their defini-tive prefixes definitive sets . We introduce our theory of definitive prefixes and sets in Section 2, including a proof that definitive sets are determined uniquely by their infinite traces, and thus that definitive sets and linear-time properties are isomorphic.

2. Definitive Prefix Sets 

In this section, we develop a theory of definitive prefixes , which we will use in Section 4 to give a semantics to LTL 3, and in Section 6 to define various classes of properties. Given a set of atomic propositions , the directly observable properties of a state of a system are the atomic propositions that hold in that state. For this reason we abstractly model a state simply as a set of atomic propositions. Let Σ be a set of such states. A trace t may be finite (in Σ∗) or infinite (in 

Σω). We denote the set of all traces, i.e. Σ∗ ∪ Σω, as Σ∞. We sometimes address a sequence a1a2⋯ of atomic propositions as a trace; formally this refers to any sequence σ1σ2⋯ ∈ Σ∞ with ai ∈ σi for all relevant i. Two traces t

and u may be concatenated in the obvious way, written as tu . If t is infinite, then tu = t. The empty trace is denoted ε. We denote the set of prefixes of a trace t as ↓ t:

↓ t ≜ { u ∣ ∃ v ∈ Σ∞. t = uv }

We also generalise this notation to sets, so ↓ X is the set of all prefixes of traces in X. The set of all extensions of a trace t is likewise written as ↑ t:

↑ t ≜ { tu ∣ u ∈ Σ∞}

The set of definitive prefixes of a set of traces X is written ☇ X. This is the set of all traces for which all extensions are a prefix of a trace in X.

☇ X ≜ { t ∣ ↑ t ⊆ ↓ X}

Equivalently, t is a definitive prefix of X iff X contains all infinite extensions of t: ☇ X = { t ∣ ↑ t ∩ Σω ⊆ X}. Intuitively, this means that ☇ X contains all those traces for which reaching X ∩ Σω is in some way inevitable , even if it hasn’t happened yet. Definitive prefixes are therefore similar to the notion of 

good and bad prefixes from Kupferman & Vardi [13], just without any moral judgement (see Section 6.2.1). A set X of traces is called definitive iff X = ☇ X. Let D ⊆ P(Σ∞) denote the set of all definitive sets. 4For example, the set {t ∈ Σ∞ ∣ ∃ uv. t = uav} (i.e., all traces that contain the state a) is definitive, as it contains all its definitive prefixes (i.e., those prefixes that contain a). For any set of traces X, we have the following straightforwardly from the definitions: • All definitive prefixes are prefixes, i.e. ☇ X ⊆ ↓ X.• The set ☇ X itself is definitive, i.e. ☇ ☇ X = ☇ X.• Any extension of a definitive prefix is also a definitive prefix, that is, 

∀t ∈ ☇ X. ↑ t ⊆ ☇ X.• The definitive prefix operator ☇ distributes over intersection, that is, 

☇(⋂ i∈I Xi) = ⋂ i∈I ☇Xi.The sets ∅ and Σ∞ are both definitive, and the definitive sets are closed under intersection, i.e., for a set of definitive sets S, ☇ ⋂ S = ⋂ S. This follows from the distributivity theorem above. The definitive sets are not closed under union, however. To see why, consider when Σ = { A, B} and the set XA

contains all traces starting with A and the set XB contains all traces starting with B. The sets XA and XB are both definitive, but their union is not: neither 

XA nor XB contain the empty trace ε, but ε ∈ ☇( XA ∪ XB), as all extensions of ε (i.e. all non-empty traces) must begin with either A or B.

4. Semantics of LTL and LTL 3

4.1. Answer-indexed Families 

We give a semantics to LTL 3 by compositionally assigning to each formula an answer-indexed family of definitive sets. In general, an answer-indexed family is a function that, given an answer (e.g. a value in B = { T, F}), produces a set of models (depending on the logic, this could be a set of states, a definitive set, a linear-time temporal property, etc.). This set contains all those models which produce the given answer for the formula in question. In this way, we invert the traditional presentation of multi-valued logics, where the truth value is the output of a satisfaction function, and instead take the desired answer a as an input and produce models as an output : a = [ σ ⊧ φ]

Φ, Ψ ∈ B → P(Σω)

5. Formula Progression 

Formula progression is a technique first introduced by Kabanza et al. 6. Properties and Monitorability 

6.1. Linear-time Temporal Properties 

Linear-time temporal properties can be broadly categorised into safety 

properties, which state that something “bad” does not happen during execu-tion, and liveness properties, which state that something “good” eventually occurs during execution [19]. Alpern & Schneider [8] formalise Lamport’s informal concepts of safety and liveness properties by defining a safety prop-erty as one that can be definitely refuted by a finite prefix of a trace, and a liveness property P as one such that any finite trace can be extended to a member of P . They define a topological space where safety properties are those sets that are (limit-)closed and liveness properties are those sets that are dense. We also see in later work [13, 20] the concept of guarantee (or 

co-safety ) properties and morbidity (or co-liveness ) properties, the comple-ments of safety and liveness properties respectively. A guarantee property can always be definitively confirmed by a finite prefix of a trace, 2 whereas any finite prefix can be extended in such a way as to refute a given morbidity 

property. Our definitive sets include those finite prefixes that can confirm (or re-fute) the property, enabling us to express these insights about finite prefixes   

> 2Thus, guarantee could be seen as an alternative formalisation of Lamport’s informal concept of a liveness property [19], different from the standard formalisation of Alpern & Schneider [8]. This formalisation of liveness is employed, e.g., by van Glabbeek [21].

19 directly. We shall use this to define a new operator on properties, which we call the guarantee kernel , which, when viewed as an interior operator, gives rise to an equivalent topology to that of Alpern & Schneider [8], enabling us to re-prove their classic result—that every property is the intersection of a safety and liveness property—by other means. 6.2. Monitorability 

There are some properties for which LTL 3 will always give a ? answer. For example, take the standard request/acknowledge format: 

φ = (r ⇒ a)

Intuitively, a property P is strong monitorable if for all traces that satisfy or violate P , the satisfaction or violation of P can be determined after only a finite observation. This corresponds to the complete monitorability of Aceto et al. [25].

7. Related Work 

7.1. Finfinite Semantics 

Aceto et al. [26] define monitoring systems essentially as two extension-closed predicates on Σ∞, acc and rej . Because they are extension closed, judgements are irrevocable , in the sense that if a monitor acc epts a finite trace, it must acc ept all extensions of that trace (and similarly for rej ). This is highly reminiscent of the two sets we use for our LTL 3 semantics (which are definitive and therefore also extension-closed), however these monitors are in the context of finfinite semantics , where properties can consist of both finite and infinite traces. Falcone et al. [27] provide definitions of monitorability in a finfinite setting which they claim correspond to safety and guarantee properties, but, as Aceto et al. [26] show, this claim is false. Aceto et al. [26] then present a hierarchy of monitorability classes that, at first glance, highly resembles that of Figure 6, but finfinite semantics differ in significant ways from our infinite trace semantics. In a finfinite context, the property ,using a weak interpretation of the operator, is not empty. Similarly, the property ⊺, using a strong interpretation of the operator, is not trivially true. Under finfinite semantics, monitors cannot make the progress assump-tion that a given finite observation will eventually be extended with another state. This significantly changes the characteristics of our monitorability classes. For example, as Aceto et al. [25] show, under finfinite semantics the only strong monitorable properties (what Aceto et al. refer to as complete monitorability ) are ∅ and Σ∞, but in infinite semantics there are non-trivial strong monitorable properties. Similarly, as noted by Aceto et al. [26], when 

Σ contains only one state σ, the property {σω}, which would be trivially true and thus strong monitorable in our infinite semantics, is not even weak monitorable (what Aceto et al. [26] call ∃PZ-monitorable ) under finfinite se-mantics. We conjecture that finfinite semantics form another dimension to 30 our lattice in Figure 6, where the finfinite semantics classes are generally strictly smaller than the infinite semantics classes, but we leave detailed ex-amination of finfinite semantics for future work. 

7.2. Syntactic Treatments 

The topological characterisations developed in this work are purely se-mantic and apply to any property L ⊆ Σω. A natural progression for future work is to consider the syntactic characterisation of these monitorable frag-ments when restricted to the class of ω-regular languages, analogous to the characterisation of the safety-progress hierarchy classes in LTL [24] or the work of Aceto et al. [25] on monitorability over finfinite semantics using sub-sets of the modal μ-calculus. Such syntactic treatments would facilitate the development of algorithms for the effective construction of monitors for specific formulae, and would allow for evaluating the completeness of existing symbolic monitoring al-gorithms against our semantic definitions. Early investigations into monitor construction for LTL used coinductive techniques [28] and term-rewriting [29] to bridge the gap between finite observations and infinite requirements. More recently, Francalanza and Cini [30] introduced a local proof system for ex-plainable monitoring based on dual-sided proof relations ( s ⊢+ φ and s ⊢− φ), which have a clear semantic analogue in our answer-indexed families. Such symbolic approaches have also been extended to real-time properties in Met-ric Temporal Logic [31]. 

7.3. Alternative Property Classes 

Havelund & Peled [32] provide a different set of property classes to the conventional hierarchy. They define six classes, AFR, AFS, SFR, SFS, NFR ,and NFS , indicating if the property is Always, Sometimes, or Never Finitely 

Refutable or Satisfiable. Safety properties coincide with AFR , guarantee prop-erties with AFS , liveness properties with NFR , and morbidity properties with 

NFS . Good monitorable properties are SFS ∪AFS , and bad monitorable prop-erties are SFR ∪AFR . The classes of weak monitorable properties and of strong monitorable properties are both Boolean combinations of other classes, so they can also be expressed in terms of Havelund & Peled’s framework sim-ilarly. BLS monitorability does not cleanly map into Havelund & Peled’s framework, and indeed they show that several of their classes contain both BLS monitorable and BLS unmonitorable properties. Havelund & Peled do not characterise any monitorability class other than BLS monitorability. 31 7.4. Revocable Semantics 

As previously mentioned, all obligation properties [24] are BLS moni-torable. If we move further up the safety-progress hierarchy [24, 33], how-ever, we quickly encounter completely unmonitorable properties, such as our

Like LTL 3 previously, the semantics of RV-LTL is presented only in terms of other logics. We believe that an inductive semantics can be designed along similar principles to that of LTL 3 given in the present paper, where our answer indexed-families instead produce four sets, two of which are definitive, rather than the two definitive sets we provide for LTL 3.As noted by O’Connor & Wickström [17], Pnueli’s finite LTL is a logic of finite completed traces, so the decision to judge partial traces as completed for the purpose of giving presumptive answers in RV-LTL is ad-hoc and 32 can produce seemingly arbitrary answers for properties higher in the safety-progress hierarchy. For example, consider a system where a flashing light consistently alternates between On and Off states: 

On Off On Off ⋯

A simple property that we might wish to monitor for this system is that the light is On infinitely often: 

On 

As this formula nests and operators, it is definitive in neither positive nor negative cases and will only give presumptive answers. But the presumptive answer given in RV-LTL depends only on the very last observed status of the light. For a trace where the light continuously alternates off and on, as above, we might intuitively say that presumptive answer ought to be true, but this formula would be considered presumptively false if our observation happens to end in a state where the light is off. Thus, the truth value obtained for this formula is rather sensitive to the point at which our finite observation ceases. 

7.5. Branching Time Monitoring 

The vast majority of research on monitoring and run-time verification concerns linear-time semantics, but we can also consider monitoring for branching-time properties. Aceto et al. [25] compare monitors for linear-time and branching-time semantics. Francalanza et al. [35, 36] provide an exten-sive investigation into branching time monitoring. Manolios & Trefler [37] and Bouajjani et al. [38] examine safety and liveness properties analogously to Alpern & Schneider [8] but in a branching-time setting. Branching-time analogues of our monitorability classes are future work. 

8. Conclusion 

We have presented a comprehensive semantic foundation for run-time verification with linear-time infinite trace semantics. By identifying those prefixes that are definitive for a property, we have given an inductive, model-based semantic accounting of LTL 3 in terms of answer-indexed families of definitive sets, and in the process shown that LTL 3 is more accurately de-scribed as a more detailed presentation of conventional LTL, rather than a distinct logic in its own right. We have formalised the popular formula 33 progression technique used in runtime verification and testing scenarios, and proved it sound and complete with respect to our semantics. Using defini-tive prefixes as a basis, we have elegantly characterised various classes of properties and their monitorability characteristics in a distinctly topological flavour, in the vein of Alpern & Schneider [8]. All of our work has been mechanised in over 2900 lines of Isabelle/HOL proof script. Our theory of definitive prefixes might provide a semantic foundation for other logics of partial traces, such as the LTL ± of Eisner et al. [39], QuickLTL from O’Connor & Wickström [17], or the aforementioned RV-LTL [6]. Our answer-indexed families may also be applicable to other multi-valued logics. Examples include rLTL [40] and the five-valued logic of Chai et al. [41]. Our monitorability characterisations are likely to have analogues in other semantic contexts such as when monitoring for branching-time properties or hyperproperties.
